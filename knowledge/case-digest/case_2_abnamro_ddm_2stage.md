# case_2_abnamro_ddm_2stage

**ABN Amro****, 2003**

ABN AMRO Bank N.V. is a Dutch state-owned bank with headquarters in Amsterdam. It was re-established, in its current form, in 2009 following the acquisition and break-up of the original ABN AMRO by a banking consortium consisting of Royal Bank of Scotland Group, Santander Group and Fortis. (In October 2007 the bank was acquired, in what was at that time the biggest bank takeover in history, by a consortium made up of the Royal Bank of Scotland Group, Fortis and Banco Santander, known as RFS Holdings B.V.). Following the collapse of Fortis, the acquirer of the Dutch business, it was nationalized by the Dutch government along with Fortis Bank Nederland. 

Back in 2003 “The Banker and Fortune Global 500” placed ABN AMRO 15th in the list of world’s biggest banks and it had operations in 63 countries, with over 110,000 employees. It was also the second-largest bank in the Netherlands and the eighth-largest in Europe by assets. ROE was 16%, with EPS of E1.85 and DPS of E0.90. A stable growth rate for the Dutch economy is 4%, the European risk free rate (in Euros) was 4.35% with a market risk premium of 4% (Netherlands is AAA rated). The bank was considered to be in a development stage, which was expected to last for 5 years. The beta of the security was slightly lower than the average firm in the market at 0.95 and was expected to change to 1, after the development period. You expect ROE to be the same with required return (cost of equity) on the stable growth stage.

**Solution**

- What method would you use for the evaluation?

Financial institutions are reluctant to release cash flow to equity information because their business model is radically different. Inevitably, the only reliable information that we can reserve to is the distributed dividends. In addition, the high retention rate indicates a high expected growth (higher than the average stable growth rate of 4%). Consequently, a 2 stage growth model would be more appropriate

The difference is massive. According to your finding, the stock is cheap in the market, so you should buy. However, judging retrospectively, this strategy would result in a loss. ABN Amro was taken over by RBS group, which collapsed shortly after. Significant part of ABN Amro was liquidated or sold on a sub-premium. The remaining part was nationalized by the Dutch government.

The vast difference between our estimation seems to be a sign of market efficiency. Market seems to evaluate the asset more accurately than we do. Potential sources of mispricing are: 

- first, ROE, which is 16% in the high growth stage, and equal to market return (remember the impact of ROE on growth rate and value), and/or, 

- second, a high expectation of growth in either the development stage (8.22%) or the stable stage (4%). Here, please note also the link between ROE, growth and PBR!!!

Judging from the premiums we are willing to pay for high and stable growth, as well as for assets in place, it seems that the main difference comes from the stable growth rate and the required return during the stable growth (which equals ROE and thus, it affects the expected growth).
